About

About the project.

Why We Built This

Everyone has opinions about what makes societies thrive. Some say it's freedom. Others say it's equality. Many point to democracy. But what does the data actually show?

We wanted to cut through the noise and let the numbers speak. So we gathered data from 150+ countries and asked a simple question: which ideas actually correlate with people living better lives?

A Note on What This Can (and Can't) Tell Us

Let's be honest: correlation isn't causation. We can't prove that economic freedom causes better outcomes. We can't run experiments on entire nations or rewind history to test different policies.

But here's the thing — when you're studying something as complex as human societies, correlation is often the best tool we have. And when you see the same patterns showing up again and again, across different metrics, from different organizations, covering different aspects of life... that's worth paying attention to.

Think of this as a compass, not a GPS. It points in a direction, even if it can't tell you exactly how to get there.

How We Did It

1

Gather the Data

We pull fresh data from trusted sources — the World Bank, UN, Heritage Foundation, and others. No cherry-picking, no outdated numbers.

2

Match the Countries

We line up countries across all datasets. If a country doesn't have data for a specific metric, we leave it out of that comparison. Simple as that.

3

Crunch the Numbers

We calculate Pearson correlations — a standard way to measure how strongly two things move together. Higher number = stronger relationship.

4

Check Our Work

We test for statistical significance (p < 0.05). This tells us whether the pattern is real or just random noise. Spoiler: the patterns here are real.

Reading the Numbers

Not sure what a correlation of 0.7 means? Here's a quick guide:

  • 0.7 - 1.0: Strong relationship (these two things really move together)
  • 0.4 - 0.6: Moderate relationship (there's definitely something here)
  • 0.1 - 0.3: Weak relationship (a hint, but not much to write home about)
  • Around 0: No relationship (these things don't seem connected)

For context: Jacob Cohen's landmark 1988 work established that in social science, r = 0.5 counts as a "large" effect (Cohen, 1988). Most published findings don't come close. The correlations here are above 0.5 — that's rare.

What do these terms mean?

Economic Freedom

Measures how free people are to work, produce, consume, and invest as they choose. It includes factors like property rights, free trade, low taxes, limited regulation, and sound money. Countries with high economic freedom have fewer government restrictions on business and personal economic decisions.

Source: Heritage Foundation Index of Economic Freedom (2024)

Equality (Gini)

Measured using the Gini Index (inverted), which captures income inequality. A Gini of 0 means perfect equality (everyone earns the same), while 100 means maximum inequality (one person has everything). We invert it so higher scores = more equality, making it comparable to the other predictors. A limitation of the Gini is that it only considers income, not accumulated wealth — this is why we also include Equality (Wealth) as a separate predictor.

Source: World Bank Gini Index (latest available per country)

Democracy

Measures the quality of democratic governance including electoral processes, civil liberties, government functioning, political participation, and political culture. Countries are scored from 0-10, with higher scores indicating stronger democratic institutions and practices.

Source: Economist Intelligence Unit Democracy Index (2023)

Equality (Palma)

Measures income inequality as the ratio of the richest 10%'s income share to the poorest 40%'s share. A Palma ratio of 1 means the top 10% earn the same as the bottom 40%. Higher values mean more inequality. We invert it for comparison so that higher scores = less inequality, like the other predictors.

Source: World Bank Income Distribution Data (latest available per country)

Equality (Wealth)

Measures the share of total net personal wealth (housing, financial assets, etc. minus debts) held by the richest 10% of the population. Unlike income-based measures like Gini or Palma, this captures wealth concentration — accumulated assets rather than annual earnings. We invert it for comparison so that higher scores = less wealth concentration.

Source: World Inequality Database (via Our World in Data) (latest available per country)

Quality of Life Outcomes

We measure quality of life using 18 metrics from trusted international sources. These cover health, wealth, safety, opportunity, well-being, and rights:

Health & Longevity

Life Expectancy — How long people live on average

Wealth & Opportunity

Understanding the Numbers

What are we measuring?

We're checking if two things tend to go together. For example: Do countries with more economic freedom also tend to have healthier people?

Reading the bars

Longer bars = stronger link to better lives.

A long bar means the pattern is strong and consistent across countries. A short bar means the link is weak or inconsistent.

What do the numbers mean?

The numbers range from 0.00 to 1.00:

0.70 - 1.00 = Strong link (they almost always go together)
0.40 - 0.69 = Moderate link (they often go together)
0.20 - 0.39 = Weak link (they sometimes go together)
0.00 - 0.19 = No real link (just random chance)

What does (n=XX) mean?

The n is the number of countries we have data for.

n=150 means 150 countries had data for both measures. More countries = more reliable results. If n is low (under 30), take the result with a grain of salt.

A real-world example

If "Economic Freedom → Life Expectancy" shows 0.59 (n=150), it means:

Across 150 countries, those with more economic freedom tend to have people who live longer. It's not a guarantee — some exceptions exist — but the pattern is clear.

Important caveat

Correlation ≠ Causation. Just because two things go together doesn't mean one causes the other. Maybe rich countries can afford both freedom AND healthcare. We're showing patterns, not proof of what causes what.
